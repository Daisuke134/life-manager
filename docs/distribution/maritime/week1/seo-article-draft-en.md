## The useful difference is who owns the next step

People use “AI assistant” and “AI agent” in different ways. There is no single label that tells you how much a product can do. Anthropic describes several meanings of “agent” and distinguishes fixed workflows from systems where a model directs its own process and tool use. The OpenAI Agents SDK describes an agent as a model configured with instructions, tools, and optional runtime behavior.

So when you compare products, ask a more practical question: after you give it one request, who decides what should happen next?

## Four questions to ask before relying on an AI agent

1. **Who chooses the next action?** Does the product wait for another prompt, or can it select a next step within the work you have delegated?
2. **Can it act outside the chat?** A tool call may create a draft, submit a form, or update a record. Having tools does not mean every action is safe or autonomous.
3. **How does it verify the result?** A sentence saying “done” is not proof that an external service accepted the action. Look for a provider confirmation, changed record, published URL, payment receipt, or another checkable result.
4. **What happens when something is uncertain?** A dependable system should show what it could not verify and avoid repeating an action that may already have happened.

These questions matter more than whether the product puts “agent” in its name. A model can answer questions well and still leave the follow-through to you. A system can also run a tool workflow without deciding what work deserves attention in the first place.

## What “manager” means in Life Manager

We use **manager** for a product design that takes continuing responsibility for delegated work: find the next useful task, choose its priority, act within the allowed boundaries, check what happened, and report the evidence. The design does not require the user to enter a task-specific goal or budget before it can begin, and Telegram is optional.

“AI manager” is our product framing, not a universal industry category. The label alone does not prove that the product can complete a job, generate income, or keep working without help. Those claims need working integrations and observable results.

For example, a person might ask a chat assistant to find freelance work. The assistant can suggest search terms and draft an application. A tool-using agent might search a marketplace and prepare the application. A manager would also need to decide whether that work is the right next task, stay within the person’s delegated limits, verify what the marketplace accepted, and report the outcome. Each step depends on the actual product and its connected accounts.

## Check the product’s current limits

Life Manager is built around work, money, and everyday life. Its public product page currently says the hosted experience focuses on Google Calendar travel-time planning, while the broader proactive cloud agent is still in development. The open-source project can be inspected and run locally, but capabilities depend on setup and integrations.

That is the current product status, not a claim of completed income-generating work. Revenue and profit are not guaranteed. A completed task, a published item, a sale recorded by a platform, and cash actually received are different results. A product should report them separately.

## A simple way to choose

- Choose an **assistant** when you want help thinking, writing, or answering a one-time question.
- Choose an **agent** when the product can use tools to carry a defined task through several steps and show what those tools returned.
- Choose a **manager-style product** when you want it to keep identifying and prioritizing delegated work, verify external outcomes, and report without a fresh daily instruction.

Before giving any product access to an account, check which actions it can take, which data it sends to providers, how to stop future work, and what evidence it uses to call a task complete.
