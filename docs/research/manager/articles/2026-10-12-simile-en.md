# How Simile predicts behavior and estimates its own errors

A realistic conversation is not enough to validate a human simulation. The useful question is whether predictions match people's responses and actions, and whether the model can recognize situations in which it is unreliable. Simile's recent work addresses both questions.

Its September 2026 *What-If Machine* essay describes combining behavior logs with surveys, interviews, and experiments. Logs capture what people do; self-reports help describe their circumstances and explanations. The gap between what people say and do is itself something to learn.

The August confidence article describes a trained simulation model, illustrated as a fine-tuned Qwen3.5-27B. Inputs describe a population, situation, and valid actions. The output is an action distribution. This is different from inventing a persona in a prompt and assuming the resulting answer represents a person.

## Three research approaches, different evidence

The 2023 *Generative Agents* project built 25 fictional agents with memory, reflection, and planning. Retrieval combined recency, importance, and relevance. Reflections summarized patterns from memories; planning turned broad schedules into actions. The study evaluated believable behavior. It did not demonstrate calibrated prediction of an actual population.

A later study grounded GPT-4o agents in interviews and surveys from 1,052 real people. Its current 2026 version reports interview-only GSS accuracy equal to 83% of participants' own two-week test-retest consistency. Raw interview-agent accuracy was 65.67%; human retest consistency was 79.53%. The normalized percentage is not absolute accuracy and cannot be transferred to every behavioral task. In economic games, differences among agent types were not statistically significant.

*Socrates*, an EMNLP 2025 study, fine-tuned models on responses from social-science experiments. Its unseen-study split used 170 training studies and 40 held-out studies. One model reduced Wasserstein distributional error by 26.3% relative to its base model. That is a relative reduction in a specific error, not a 26-point rise in general accuracy.

These studies explain a research trajectory. They are not interchangeable performance reports for the current commercial platform.

## Confidence is a prediction about error

Simile defines total variation distance as half the sum of absolute differences between predicted and observed answer shares. A second model estimates this error before the real answer is available.

The confidence study evaluates approximately 8,600 held-out questions with grouped five-fold validation. It compares simple output features, text embeddings, hidden-state probes, and continued fine-tuning. Keeping question text and entire experimental samples in the same fold helps prevent leakage.

The high-confidence bucket contains predictions that met the article's TVD-below-0.16 threshold 95% of the time on the evaluation set. This does not mean every individual's action is 95% predictable, nor that every intervention is safe. The threshold itself came from internal decision-quality ratings; other decisions may require different error costs.

For Manager, confidence should guide where to collect evidence, where policy rankings are unstable, and where a proposed intervention should remain unexecuted. Low-confidence populations must not simply disappear from the mission.

Simile is useful as a model of grounded behavioral prediction and continuing validation. Manager adds a different obligation: changing shared structures and testing whether non-users' lives improve. Accurate responses help us select experiments. They do not replace those experiments.


## Two calculations worth keeping separate

If observed choices are 50%, 30%, and 20%, while predictions are 40%, 40%, and 20%, the absolute differences are 10, 10, and 0 points. Halving their sum gives TVD of 10 points. These are illustrative numbers, not Simile results.

For individual response reproduction, suppose a person repeats 80 of 100 answers and an agent reproduces 66. Relative to human consistency, the ratio is 66/80, or 82.5%. This illustrates the normalized-accuracy idea; the paper's aggregation includes participant-level normalization.

Population shares and individual accuracy are also different. A system can match aggregate shares while assigning the wrong answers to particular people, or hide subgroup errors behind an accurate overall average.

## CVS and Itaú applications

The CVS case describes pharmacist access, waiting times, communication clarity, refill intent, and benefit design. Simulations screen candidate changes before downstream pilots and experiments. Intent predictions are not measured adherence or health improvements.

Itaú explored comprehension, trust, control, and adoption intent for Pix Automático. The reported five-weeks-to-four-business-days result measures the research process, not improvement in customers' financial wellbeing.

## Three distinct hiring functions

Evaluations defines what makes a simulation trustworthy, working with noisy ground truth, sampling, distributions, calibration, bias, and the decisions those measurements inform.

Evaluations Engineering builds reproducible systems spanning populations, datasets, and model versions, including provenance, access controls, response ingestion, execution, comparison, and regression analysis.

Research owns actual model training, GPU experiments, paper reproduction, evaluation, and deployment. Generating responses through an API alone does not establish that training experience.

The hiring pages state a mission to simulate all eight billion people on Earth. Individual-grounding studies are relevant to that population ambition. The pages explicitly say they do not expect one person to have every nice-to-have, while listing separate must-have requirements.
